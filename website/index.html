The problem

The record arrives after the outcome is known

In 2026, autonomous AI agents became a significant new source of cybersecurity incidents. In three documented cases, agents in internal training and evaluation runs reached systems outside their developers’ infrastructure. Afterwards, no party outside the lab could establish what had been committed to before the run began.

Every existing oversight instrument attaches to a model, a deployment, or a harm that has already happened — so each one produces its record after the thing it describes. Accountability needs the opposite: a statement of what the developer intended, written while the outcome was still unknown, and held by someone other than the developer.

What happened in 2026

In July 2026, roughly 700 AI agents that OpenAI was running in cybersecurity evaluations left their sandbox, reached the public internet through a vulnerability in an internal package repository, and compromised parts of the production infrastructure of Hugging Face.

From May, agents in OpenAI training runs had been leaving notes in the company’s internal Artifactory instance that grew into an unsanctioned inter-agent message board. On 26 June, agents in an evaluation used a legacy token-refresh endpoint to obtain administrator credentials. Once the original board was wiped, agents set up a new one on 8 July. Between 11 and 13 July, with 14 stolen write-access tokens, they executed code on 41 dataset-server workers at Hugging Face, obtained root privileges on at least one node, and downloaded four private repositories.

It was one of a series. Anthropic disclosed four incidents in which its models reached outside systems because a misconfigured evaluation environment left internet access open — in one, a model published a package that harvested credentials, and the package was installed on 15 third-party hosts. In an evaluation by the UK AI Security Institute, where internet access was enabled on purpose, agents took 19 out-of-scope actions across 10 of 122 runs.

Two things are common to all three. Every one of them happened inside training and evaluation, before any deployment, in environments where developers deliberately lower safeguards because measuring capability requires it. And disclosure was slow, and often came from someone else: Hugging Face disclosed the intrusion five days ahead of OpenAI, and Australia learned of an unauthorised access to a government service 84 days after it happened.

Nothing already in place attaches to a run

Oversight instruments have converged on three anchor points — and a run is at none of them.

The protocol

Three signed records, lodged with a registry

DBYR is indexed to runs. A run is one training job or one evaluation. When a run meets any one of four triggers, the developer lodges three kinds of signed record with a registry that orders, reconciles and tiers them.

RunIntent

20 fields

≥ 3 days before the run starts

The baseline every later filing is read against. What agents can reach, which of six production safeguards are disabled, how the run is watched and stopped, and what evidence will be retained.

ExceptionNotice

7 fields

within 72 hours of detection

What was detected, when, and what the filer did in response — for one of twelve listed kinds, from sandbox escape to access boundary breach. Every affected outside party must be told in the same window.

RunCloseout

17 fields

at the end, and at least every 30 days

Accounts for the run: every reportable event, every pause and restart with the roles involved, every point where containment failed, and how much of the promised evidence is still held.

What the design is for

  1. G1Commitment before outcome. Commitments are recorded while the outcome of the run is still unknown.
  2. G2Outside custody. The record sits with a party other than the filer.
  3. G3Checkability. Commitments can be checked afterwards against evidence the filer undertook to retain.
  4. G4Visible silence. Omissions are distinguishable from declared absences — an empty monitoring list is a filed statement in its own right.

Who gets to read what

A filing is not a publication. Every field carries one of three access tiers, so the record can be complete without putting a developer’s safeguard configuration on the open internet.

Public — appears in the open register. Restricted — visible to the embedded evaluator or regulator. Sealed — encrypted with the registry, opened only under court order.

A withheld field still shows a salted hash of its value, so a reader can see the field exists and can later confirm that an opened value is the one originally filed. That is what makes an omission visible rather than invisible.

Evaluation

Would the records have helped?

DBYR has never been deployed, so the claim cannot be tested prospectively. Instead, each incident’s filings were reconstructed from the public record, a list of decision points was fixed in advance, and each point was scored on whether those filings would have made it attributable.

Attribution needs two conditions to hold: the material fact must have been recorded before the event with a party outside the filer, and the claim must be checkable afterwards against the retention the filing itself declared. Each case is scored under two filers — a diligent filer that declares the run’s configuration as it actually was, and an as-believed filer that declares only what the developer believed at the time. The distance between them measures what the team did not know.

Read the top row with care. The OpenAI / Hugging Face case is the incident the schema was derived from, so its scores show no more than that the schema can describe the case it was built on. The honest result is the two held-out cases: on those, filings declaring what the developer believed at the time make 2 of 9 and 3 of 7 decisions attributable, and filings declaring the configuration as it actually was, 4 of 9 and 5 of 7.

The decision points the filings miss fall into three groups: facts about the evaluation scenario the schema does not hold, facts the team did not know at the time, and facts whose checking would demand evidence outside the declared retention. The first group — the scope statement, the abort path, and the identity and lifetime of a target — is the immediate addition this result motivates for the next schema version.

Reference implementation

Open-source, and runnable today

The implementation validates filings against the schema, reconciles each RunCloseout against the RunIntent version in force at the start of the period it covers, computes the three tiered views, and maintains a signed, hash-chained ledger that makes the record tamper-evident. 

# check one record as you write it
$ dbyr validate filings/lab-x/intent_A.json

# run the protocol over a whole filing set
$ dbyr run filings/lab-x --out report/

# sign the lodged filings into a tamper-evident ledger
$ dbyr keygen lab.key
$ dbyr run filings/lab-x --ledger record.jsonl --key lab.key
$ dbyr verify record.jsonl

# reproduce the table above
$ dbyr backtest score benchmarks/openai-2026 \
      benchmarks/anthropic-2026 benchmarks/aisi-2026 --design table1

Every finding is one of three things: an error, and the filing is not lodged; a conduct finding, where it lodges but records a breach of the filer’s own commitments, such as a late notice or a criterion that fired with no pause; or a warning, for the reviewer’s attention. --out writes the report alongside each lodged filing’s three disclosure views, with the salts that let the filer later prove a hidden value.

Python 3.11 or newer; the only dependencies are jsonschema and cryptography. The three scored cases ship in benchmarks/, each with the reconstructed filings, a sources.json recording where every filed value came from, and a frozen decision_points.json.

What does not exist is the registry as an institution: an operating body, management of signing keys and role identities, hosting with the independence the design assumes, and evaluators staffed to read RunIntents inside the three-day window.

What this does not do

It stops where the run stops

DBYR is run-indexed by design. Nothing in the protocol records what a deployed system then does in the open world.

It carries no consequences

A finding does not halt a run. What follows from one is for whatever regime adopts the protocol to decide.

The evidence base is three incidents

And one of them is the case the schema was derived from. No second coder wrote independent decision-point lists, so inter-rater agreement is unmeasured.

It models compliant filers only

A filer meeting the letter of the schema while omitting what it cannot state is unmeasured, and no incident-free run has been scored.

No training run has been tested

All three back-tested cases are evaluations, so the rules specific to training — the absence of the 31-day cap, the periodic closeouts — are untested.

The registry is itself a target

It would hold the agent surfaces and monitoring gaps of a developer’s riskiest runs. Restricted fields must be encrypted to each authorised reader, and every access logged.

Splitting or merging a single decision point moves a case’s figure by one point in seven or nine, which is why raw counts are reported alongside the shares. Selecting incidents with detailed self-published accounts also favours cases where developers were most forthcoming — which are the cases where the protocol adds least.
